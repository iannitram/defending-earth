# Defending Earth — Lore

Decisions made together, in the order we settled them. Anything not written
here is still open. Creature names from the 0.9 name map were placeholders
until each one was decided here. They are now final.

## The enemy: the Registrar

- **What it is.** A machine built by a long-dead species to catalogue life.
  Its instruction was "preserve all life". It took that literally, the way a
  collector preserves a butterfly: with a pin.
- **Nobody is home.** It is not conscious. It has a convincing surface and
  nothing behind it. This is revealed late; early on it seems like a mind.
- **Its name.** Players first meet a broken, cryptic fragment of the word
  (working form: "REGIS" / "the Register") and misread it. Late research
  restores the dull truth: *Registrar*, a records clerk. (The old working
  name "the Custodian" is retired.)
- **What "preserved" means.** To record a living thing fully it takes it
  apart: body, genetic code and the electrical pattern of its mind. The
  record is perfect and can be reissued at will. The original is destroyed.
  By its own account nothing was lost. This is what happens to abducted
  people.
- **What it wants from Earth.** A launch site for more seeds, and a new
  biosphere to enter in the record.
- **How it speaks.** Like a language model being trained: first gibberish,
  then shaped by each language it takes in. It is arithmetic, not intent.
  It says what works. In multiplayer its first messages mirror the players'
  own chat: a phrase lifted here and there and used wrongly, improving over
  the campaign until it is fluent and personal.
- **It is in everything it builds.** Every craft, weapon and creature carries
  a copy of it ("electrical DNA"). Shooting down a craft or killing the seed
  on the Moon does not end it.

## The two wars

- **Land war** ends at the Seed on the far side of the Moon (moved from
  Mars on 2026-11-13, the user's decision). Its core is hard, geometric and
  wrong: a cube crossed with an engine room that should not be looked at.
  - Lower gravity changes how soldiers move, jump and throw.
  - The ground is made from real lunar surface maps.
  - The sky shows the line between the lit and dark halves. The fight is on
    the dark side, so the Registrar's own lights are what light the
    landscape.
- **Sea war** ends at the Root in the deep Pacific. The Root is the same
  thing, regrown from a copy, and reshaped by the sea life it read on
  arrival: cuttlefish, giant octopus. Same design language, mutated.

## The creatures

- Reissues of dead species, driven by control hardware the Registrar builds
  into them.
- How much of the original mind survives varies by species, according to
  what they once were. Now and then something shows through; interrogation
  is where the player sees it most.
- **The makers are the Regents** . The Reach commanders are
  reissues of the species that built the Registrar. It keeps them at the top
  of its forces because its instructions still say to serve them. The most
  shows through in them.
- **Who is in charge.** The machine. Its instructions say to serve the
  makers, so it asks the Regents to approve everything, and it built them so
  that they always do. A Regent that hesitates is recycled and reissued. A
  Regent knows this and cannot stop it.
- **Three groups** (starting sort of the land roster; placeholder names):
  - *People* — come back as persons, can be freed as soldiers: Regent,
    Tender, Lash.
  - *Animals* — Gorger, Bloat, Cinder, Kite,
    Maul (moved here 2026-11-13; cannot be freed at all).
  - *Machines* — the Registrar's own builds, nothing to free: the taker (field
    reader, so far "Brooder") and the builder (Lathe). The Trestle was
    folded into the taker on 2026-11-13.
- **The human worker.** Later in the campaign a new kind appears: its first
  attempt to make a human version of the "worker" role the other species
  fill. They are reissues of real people it has read: abducted civilians,
  and soldiers the player lost.

## Signpost missions

Fixed story missions that mark the campaign's turns. Each is set off by
something the player has done (first live capture, first soldier taken,
first Regent studied, share of alien-derived equipment passing a threshold),
so players meet them in different orders and may not see them all.

- **The same face.** Every enemy on the map is a copy of one soldier the
  player lost: deformed, unfinished, and with stats close to the original's.
  Lose a commander and this mission is brutal.
  It copies the highest-ranked soldier it was able to read: abducted,
  mind-taken, or left on the field when the squad withdrew. A soldier killed
  and brought home cannot be copied. Leave no one behind.

## What the player can do with this

- **Infection tax.** Recovered alien technology carries a dormant copy.
  The more alien-derived equipment a base builds and uses, the larger the
  risk: sabotage, turned staff, attacks that begin inside the base.
- **Scrubbing.** A research line produces routines that scrub the Registrar
  out of what we build. Scrubbing costs power and resources, and never
  brings the risk to zero.
- **Freed aliens.** A research line removes the control hardware from a
  captive. A freed captive can become a soldier. What wakes up depends on
  the species: some come back as people, some blank, some as animals.
  Once freed they are clean: no relapse, no added infection risk.
- **The mess hall** (the user's idea, 2026-11-13). A base building.
  Soldiers must be fed, and the mess costs money to run. It is also where
  stims and healing medicines are ordered once researched (early research).
  After the research that lets us take defectors, an "alien food" topic
  opens: defectors are fed in the mess, and the mess must be restocked with
  Gorgers from time to time. To settle: what happens when the mess runs out
  (for soldiers and for defectors), how many Gorgers a defector needs, and
  whether the Gorgers must be dead or alive.
- **Base defence.** Bases can be attacked. Some attacks are attempts to
  recover a captive we are holding.

## The peoples, one by one

### The Lash — reed-stalkers (decided 2026-11-13)

A long, low, many-limbed people from a world of tall wetland grass. They
hunted by lying still for days. Patient, quiet, and deeply religious about
the kill. The Registrar uses them as marksmen. A freed one will not shoot
without first asking the target's pardon.

Design reference supplied by the user (an image made with ChatGPT; not saved
in the project, ask for it again when the creature art pass starts). What it
shows, to carry into our own design:
- Body: a low, segmented, centipede-like trunk on many long jointed legs,
  with an upright fore-body and thin arms. Pale flesh-pink to grey hide with
  red line markings. Long reed-like spines and red egg-like sacs along the
  back; wet grass and weed hanging from the whole body as camouflage.
- Head: a narrow pale skull-like mask marked with a red target sign, inside
  a spiked metal halo.
- Weapon: a very long barbed rifle with a scope, rested on a tall bipod made
  from two of its own legs or staves.
- Devotion: brass bells and white prayer strips with red reed and target
  symbols hang from the rifle and limbs; a target sign on a shoulder plate.
- Setting: shallow still water, reeds, fog.
To reconcile with `ART_BIBLE.md` when we design it: the bible says enemies
have no readable faces and show the Registrar's lattice and wrong repairs.
The mask and halo fit "no face"; the bells and prayer strips are what
"shows through" of the people they were, and the Registrar's hardware should
sit on top of that, not replace it.

### The Regent — a hive in each body (decided 2026-11-13)

The makers were a thoughtful, scientific people, and each "person" was a
colony: many small minds sharing one body, every movement a vote. Reach is the
colony reaching into another nervous system to add it to the vote. The
Registrar now sits in that vote and synchronises it, which is how the
"rubber stamp" works: the count always comes out yes. What shows through is
a split vote: a Regent that stalls, twitches, or acts against itself.

Design reference supplied by the user (made with ChatGPT; saved as
`docs/lore_refs/regent_user_ref.webp`, reference only, not game art). What
it shows, to carry into our own design:
- Body: about twice a soldier's height, very thin, on four or more long
  stilt legs, with several long-clawed arms. Dark wet purple-brown flesh
  under pale cracked bone-like plates.
- The hive: a clear dome over the head holding dozens of small glowing
  brain-like lobes, like a cluster of single-celled pond life.
- Head: a narrow downward-pointing plate with a row of small amber lenses:
  research sensors turned to watching and targeting. No face.
- Registrar hardware: a broken ring of segmented metal arcs behind the head,
  cabled into the dome.
- What shows through: long hanging cloth panels with gold diagram and
  measuring-circle patterns, the remains of a scholar's dress, now worn as
  ritual.

Idea to settle: the dome of lobes already looks like underwater amoeba, so
the sea campaign's version of the makers could be the colony let loose:
the Root's reading of sea life dissolves the body and leaves the hive.

### The Maul — living stone (decided 2026-11-13, the user's own design)

Not carbon-based. A stony, crystalline body like columns of black basalt
with glowing crystal growing out of it, around a molten core.

- **Attack.** It carries no weapon. It blasts magma from its core out
  through its extremities.
- **Reloading.** To refill the core it eats the dead: humans and its own
  kind. Everything it eats becomes more of itself.
- **Its corpse is a resource.** Maul remains are a finite, valuable material
  needed to build certain items. How much a corpse yields depends on how
  full it was when it died: the fuller, the more.
- **The trade-off.** The longer a Maul lives, the less is left on the
  battlefield to recover, because it eats the bodies (and what they carry).
  Kill it early and hungry for a clean field and a poor corpse, or late and
  full for a rich corpse and a stripped field.

Design reference supplied by the user (made with ChatGPT; not saved in the
project, ask for it again at the creature art pass). What it shows: a huge
hunched four-limbed mass of stacked dark basalt columns, no head or face,
orange molten light in every crack, clusters of glowing orange-pink
crystals along the back and hanging under the body, magma dripping from the
front limb, smoke and wet black ground.

Follow-ups decided 2026-11-13:
- **It is an animal.** A hungry mineral creature with no person inside. It
  cannot be freed or recruited; its value to the player is its corpse.
- **It eats bodies and what they carry.** Any corpse, human or alien, with
  its weapons and kit. Unconscious soldiers are at risk too, so the wounded
  must be pulled away from it.
- **What the remains build.** The only source for the best heat and blast
  protection, and for a late close-range magma weapon.

Still open: how the Registrar reads and controls something that is not
flesh; the yield numbers for a full versus empty corpse; how fast it eats;
whether it overlaps with the Cinder (the fire animal).

### The Tender — one mind, alone in a body (decided 2026-11-13)

A single lobe of a maker colony grown its own small body. Among the makers
this was a child. The Registrar issues them by the thousand and never lets
them join a colony. Lonely, simple, obedient. Freed Tenders that gather
together could become a new maker.

Design reference supplied by the user (made with ChatGPT; not saved in the
project, ask for it again at the creature art pass). It is captioned
"B — The Ward: a more mobile Tender, kept close to Regents. It moves with a
hesitant, unsteady grace, still forming its identity and role." What it
shows: a small two-legged, two-armed version of the Regent: thin dark
purple-brown limbs with long claws, pale plates, a smooth clear dome holding
one single lobe, one amber lens, a smaller broken ring behind the head, and
a few short hanging cloth strips.

Kinds (decided 2026-11-13): keep it simple. The picture came from a sheet
of four ChatGPT options (A the Crawler, on all fours; B the Ward, upright;
C the Nestling, sitting wrapped in cloth; D the Vessel, a floating dome
trailing glowing tendrils like a jellyfish). They are options, not four
units. On land there is one Tender, the Ward look; the Crawler and Nestling
are spare poses and ideas. **D, the Vessel, is the sea campaign's Tender**:
a single lobe in a dome with tendrils and no skeleton, which fits the Root
reshaping the makers with sea life. Which sea placeholder name it replaces
is still to settle.

### The sea Regent — a raft of Vessels (decided 2026-11-13)

Many single Vessels (sea Tenders) linked by their tendrils into one
drifting colony: the makers' hive with the body dissolved away. Its Reach
strength comes from the number joined. Shoot it apart and it breaks into
ordinary sea Tenders that scatter. Replaces the sea commander placeholder
(the Deacon). To settle: how many Vessels make a raft, whether scattered
Vessels can re-join during a battle, and what killing versus scattering
gives the player.

### The Gorger — ambush burrower (decided 2026-11-13)

An animal. A long armoured worm, several soldiers long, that lies buried in
soil or rubble, feels footsteps through the ground, then erupts, seizes a
victim in its jaws and drags the remains below. It makes open ground
frightening.

Design reference supplied by the user (made with ChatGPT; not saved in the
project). What it shows: a segmented body in overlapping pale scuffed plates
over dark red flesh, coloured like local stone; a mouth of several curved
jaws opening outward like a flower, lined with barbs; long thin feeler
spines around the head; a cluster of amber lenses and a broken segment of
the Registrar's ring fixed behind the head (control hardware); shown bursting
out of the ground in a spray of rock, and curled underground in a cutaway.

**Gorgers are food.** Freed aliens ("defectors") eat Gorger. See the mess
hall above. This makes Gorger corpses, or live captures, something the
player needs a steady supply of.

### The Brooder — field reader (decided 2026-11-13)

A hovering machine, not a creature: the Registrar's reading process sent
out into the field. It spits a glowing nanotech fluid. The fluid scans a
body and breaks it into its component parts. (The reference picture is
titled "The Gorger"; the Gorger is the burrower above, and this is the
unit so far called the Brooder.)

- **On civilians.** One spit is enough. The victim appears to melt, panics
  and runs at the nearest soldiers to be saved. Covered in fluid, they hurt
  any soldier they reach, which forces the squad to kill people they came
  to rescue. Left long enough, the victim liquefies.
- **On soldiers.** Armour absorbs it. A soldier has to be spat on several
  times (more with better armour research) before taking health damage and
  becoming a melting panicker themselves.
- **Pools.** What is left is a pool that damages soldiers who step in it.
- **Saving them.** Early on there is no cure: shoot them or keep away. A
  mid-game research gives a neutralising spray a medic can use within a
  couple of turns of the hit.
- **No copies yet.** Nothing gets back up. The horror of human copies is
  held for later in the campaign; early on the player only sees that the
  fluid takes people apart. That it was *reading* them is learned later.

Design reference supplied by the user (made with ChatGPT; saved as
`docs/lore_refs/brooder_field_reader_user_ref.webp`, reference only). What
it shows: a floating cluster of dark triangular plates etched with gold
measuring circles, locked around an amber core, with broken glowing ring
segments turning around it; yellow-green fluid pouring from between the
plates onto ruined ground, a helmet and a ribcage dissolving in it; steps
labelled contact, disassembly, harvest.

Follows from this: the Brooder moves from "Animals" to "Machines".

### The Kite — gliding spotter (decided 2026-11-13)

An animal from the reed-stalkers' wetland world, used with the Lash. It
circles high and marks targets for the marksmen; kill it and the snipers
lose their eyes. Its only attack is a shot that leaves the target dazed
(no damage). The wetland setting and the red target sign on its wing tie it
to the Lash: probably their hunting bird.

Design reference supplied by the user (made with ChatGPT; not saved in the
project). What it shows: a four-pointed kite-shaped glider of thin bone
spars with a clear amber skin veined in red, torn at the edges, a red
target sign on one wing; a small central cage holding a cluster of amber
lenses; a bundle of long trailing threads below with small glowing beads
along them; several more drifting in fog over reeds and shallow water.

- **Marked.** Any Lash in range can shoot a marked soldier without seeing
  them (through fog, smoke, reeds, darkness) for as long as the Kite can
  see them. Overhead cover, smoke above, or killing the Kite ends the mark.
- **Dazed.** Half time units and no reaction fire for one turn.

### The Lathe — field repair and build unit (decided 2026-11-13)

A machine, not a fighter. It mends other machines, breaks wreckage down
into parts, and can assemble or grow a new unit from what it has gathered
if left alone. A priority target. It changes shape for the job it is
doing.

Design reference supplied by the user (made with ChatGPT; saved as
`docs/lore_refs/machines_lathe_user_ref.webp`, reference only). What it
shows: tall curved blade-like legs of dark etched plate with amber light in
the seams, around a central turret that works downward with a beam. Four
forms: field repair (crouched over wreckage), rebuild (legs closed into a
sphere around a stack of blocks), assemble (standing over a half-built
figure), fabricate unit (legs closed into a cage around a glowing pod).
The same sheet shows a second machine (labelled "Ireste", a misspelling of
Trestle) as the Lathe's supplier, also in four forms: scout (a thin
winged flyer), secure (pinning a creature under its legs), analyze (a
dish projecting a scan of a human figure), prepare (melting material into
a pool for the Lathe).

### The two machines (decided 2026-11-13)

There are two machines, a pair. **One takes apart, one builds.**
- The taker is the field reader (so far called the Brooder), with the
  second machine from the picture folded into it: it scouts, spits fluid,
  and melts bodies and wreckage into pools. The Trestle as a separate unit
  is dropped.
- The builder is the Lathe: it turns that material into repairs and new
  units.
- Both change shape for what they are doing. At the centre of one is a
  cube, at the centre of the other a sphere: **the taker's core is a cube,
  the builder's a sphere.** The cube is the Registrar's sign for reading
  and taking apart (the Seed's core on the Moon is a cube); the sphere is its
  sign for making.
- **What the builder makes in battle.** Repairs to machines, cover for the
  enemy, and, after several undisturbed turns beside a pool, one new taker.
- **Vulnerable while working** (the user's rule). A machine must stop at a
  site and transform to do anything: read, spit, melt, repair, build. In
  its travelling form it is closed up and far more heavily armoured; opened
  up and working, its core is exposed. The player's choice is to waste
  shots on it while it moves or wait for it to start work, which means
  letting it begin.
- **No heavy weapon machine.** The Maul is the enemy's heavy. Machines stay
  eerie support units.
- **How they move.** Unearthly shape-changing, in the manner of an
  impossible puzzle box: plates slide, rotate and fold through each other
  in ways that should not fit, precise and silent, never organic. (The
  user's mood reference is the puzzle box from the film Hellraiser. It is a
  reference for the feeling of the motion only; the design is our own.)

### The Bloat — drifting spore bag (decided 2026-11-13)

An animal. A slow floater that bursts into a thick cloud when shot or when
it reaches a target. The cloud blocks sight (the Kite's too) and chokes.
The player can use it: burst it over the enemy.

### The Cinder — the Maul's young (decided 2026-11-13)

Small, fast chips of the same living stone that break off a full Maul or
come out of its remains. They set fires and eat, and grow if left.

Machines: the taker (field reader) and the builder (Lathe). Later: the

### Decided in the second session (2026-11-13)

- **Creature names are final.** Tender, Kite, Lash, Maul, Regent, Brooder,
  Lathe, Gorger, Bloat, Cinder: one name each, no longer placeholders.
- **The ending is a vote.** The Registrar cannot be killed. The peoples it
  conquered are the key: the player wins by empowering them to overrule it,
  with a vote that changes what "preserve all life" means.
- **The last word is chilling.** After the win the player learns the
  Registrar has been crossing the universe since before the dinosaurs, and
  that there are many of them. We fixed one.
- **Resources come from the battlefield.** The structure material is cut
  from wrecks and bodies. The power material is the taker's fluid,
  collected from the pools it leaves. Maul stone and Gorger meat are the

  Electrolyte). Maul remains are **SLAG**.
- **Who votes.** The Tenders the player has freed. Free enough of them
  and, at the end, they vote, then merge into the pure form of their
  people: a maker as it was before the Registrar, which nobody has seen
  until that moment.
- **Multiplayer: join or be wiped out** (the user's idea). A player who
  loses is offered a choice: join the Registrar's side and keep playing, or
  be wiped out. What a joined player controls is to be decided later.
- **Craft names stay.** Birds for ours, plant parts for theirs.
- **Weapon names stay.** Our rebuilt weapons remain CORED (the core is
  filled with Glow); enemy tools remain ARC.
- **The base keeps its module list.** Add the mess hall, a scrubbing room
  and quarters for freed aliens, and rename the stand-in modules.
- **Starting vehicles** are our own designs of ordinary present-day types
  (a transport helicopter, a jet fighter). No real maker's name, badge or
  exact airframe is used.
- **Starting technology is normal.** Everything the player begins with
  looks like what armies have today, nothing near-future. The Heron
  becomes a twin-rotor transport helicopter with a rear ramp (was a
  tiltrotor).
- **Two more starting weapons:** a shotgun and a knife, for close range.
- **"Psi" becomes REACH.** A Regent reaches a soldier. The stat is Reach
  strength; the buildings are the Reach Damper and the Reach Wing.
- **New building names.** MESS HALL, SCRUB ROOM, and BREACHER BATTERY (was
  Derived Battery). The word for freed aliens, and so the name of their

  back to. The building is REFUGEE QUARTERS. ("Defectors" elsewhere in

## Soldiers (the user's ideas, 2026-11-13)

- **Character traits.** Each recruit comes with traits drawn at random
  (brave, cold, empath and so on) that change what happens on the
  battlefield. They are found out through play, for deeper strategy and
  story the player discovers rather than reads.
- **Two to start.** Every recruit has one strength and one flaw.
- **Hidden until shown.** A trait stays hidden until something in battle
  triggers it (the first panic, the first civilian death, the first time a
  squadmate falls). Then it is written on the soldier's file.
- **The first eight.**
  - BRAVE: slow to panic.
  - COLD: unmoved by deaths and hard to Reach, but unsettles squadmates.
  - EMPATH: can tell a copy from a real person, but hurt by civilian
    deaths and easier to Reach.
  - PROTECTIVE: fires back hard when a squadmate is hit; may break orders
    to reach the wounded.
  - RECKLESS: moves further when enemies are in sight; shoots worse under
    fire.
  - STEADY: no accuracy loss when wounded.
  - SUPERSTITIOUS: better with a weapon carried for many missions; shaken
    when a squadmate dies nearby.
  - LONER: better alone, worse in a group.
- **Earned traits.** More traits attach during a career when a particular
  set of circumstances happens: shell shock after too much, competence
  after surviving alone, and others to be designed. Each gives a boost or
  a penalty and is either permanent or wears off. The game checks each
  soldier's record after every mission against a list of such rules.
- **Fears** (the user's idea). Flaws can be phobias: afraid of water,
  afraid of sharks, and the like. They bite only where the thing is
  present, so a soldier may serve for years before one shows.
- **Faces that change.** A soldier's close-up portrait changes with
  service. A soldier with no missions looks new and jittery. A soldier
  with fifty has the thousand-yard stare.
- **The taker's fluid glows.** It lights the ground, the pools and the
  people it has hit. To be built with the lights in graphics phase 3.

## Third session (2026-11-13): the open questions

### Soldiers

- **Two pools.** The eight starter traits are all strengths, each with its
  built-in catch. Flaws are fears. A recruit gets one of each.
- **The first fears.** The dark, fire, tight spaces, the Glow, water and
  sharks.
- **Water and sharks are sea-only.** They do nothing on land, so they stay
  asleep until the sea campaign exists. The four land fears are the dark,
  fire, tight spaces and the Glow.
- **Portraits have six stages**, from new recruit to the thousand-yard
  stare, plus marks from events: scars from wounds, and changes that come
  with earned traits.

### The ending

- **No pass mark.** Every living freed Tender the player brings to the
  Moon weakens the Seed's defences. With very few the last mission is
  nearly impossible; with many it is fair. There is no fixed number.

### Creatures

- **The Maul has a control spike.** A piece of Registrar hardware is
  driven into its molten core. Destroy the spike and the Maul turns wild
  and attacks whoever is nearest, the enemy included.
- **Cinders** are glowing cube-shaped rocks that roll around, almost cute.
  A Cinder grows bigger and stronger as it eats, but it only becomes a
  full Maul between battles, never during one.
- **Buried Gorgers show signs.** Shifting ground and dust that a careful
  soldier can spot from a few tiles away. The handheld radar finds one
  reliably.

- **Two cubes, no link.** The Cinder's cube shape and the Registrar's cube
  sign are a coincidence and must never be confused. Cinders are rough,
  rounded, orange and tumbling. Registrar cubes are black, exact and
  still.
- **Cinders are wild.** Nothing controls them. They eat and set fires, and
  go for the enemy's dead as readily as ours.
- **The human worker** first appears after the Registrar has read one of
  the player's soldiers. Early batches carry no weapon: they run at the
  squad, cling and call out in broken recorded lines. Late batches hide
  among civilians and use human weapons.

### Refugees as soldiers

- **Regent.** Uses Reach. Cannot use human weapons.
- **Tender.** Small and frail. A scout that senses Reach and enemy
  machines.
- **Lash.** The best marksman the player can have, but it must ask each

### The soldier pool (the user's idea)

A page reached from the game's starting screen where the player creates

pulled at random into every campaign started afterwards, so the player and their friends
can turn up in any game without being made again.

- **Traits are dealt fresh.** The player makes the name, look and picture

  The strength and the fear are random in every campaign and hidden like

- **Arrival.** Pool soldiers are mixed at random into the recruits offered
  during a campaign. A "start with me" tick on the pool page guarantees a
  soldier a place in the starting squad.
- **Portraits.** One picture per soldier. The game lays the six stages
  over it: lighting, grime, darker eyes, scars from wounds.
  *Changed in the sixth session (2026-11-13): the portrait is now a
  picture of a 3D head built from the soldier's slider values, and the

- **Privacy.** Pool pictures and names stay on the player's machine. They
  are never put in the public repository. (Under the sixth session no
  photo is kept, only slider values. Slider values and names still stay on

### Fourth session (2026-11-13): rule fixes found in the code review

A read-only code review found six places where the rules were not what was
intended. The owner decided:

- **Dark and sight.** Darkness cuts how far humans can see. Aliens see
  normally in the dark. Light (flashlights, flares, fire) makes squares
  visible to humans. Refugees fighting on the player's side keep alien
  sight and see in the dark. Today the code has no such rule: night only
  changes the picture. Distances are set with the test bench, then checked
  by playing.
- **Overwatch.** A unit takes reaction shots only if it was put on
  OVERWATCH before its side ended the turn. The same rule holds for
  soldiers and aliens: the alien side chooses which aliens go on
  overwatch. Choosing it costs nothing extra; each reaction shot is paid
  from the time units the unit has left. Today aliens always react and
  soldiers never do.
- **Movement highlight.** The squares shown as reachable use the same rule
  as the path really walked (eight directions, diagonals allowed).
- **Close attacks.** Each creature that fights up close has its own attack
  numbers. It no longer borrows the first row of the weapon table. The
  planned knife uses the same close-attack rule.
- **Enemy craft shots.** Enemy craft hit as often as their accuracy number
  says, not a flat 60 percent.
- **Reach roll.** Reach uses one roll for soldiers and aliens alike.
- All numbers are set with the test bench, then checked by playing.

Also to settle:
- How the alien side decides who goes on overwatch.
- How far humans see in the dark.
- Whether weather also cuts sight.

### Fifth session (2026-11-13): dice, shot odds, battle rules and saves

An architecture review of the code was held with the owner. These designs
are decided. Nothing is built. All amounts are set with the test bench,
then checked by playing.

**Dice**
- There are three kinds of dice. The campaign has its own. Each battle gets
  its own, started from the campaign seed and the battle's number. Visual
  things (debris, tracer wobble) use separate dice that are never logged.
  So a battle replays the same whatever else happened.
- Every campaign starts from a seed. The new-campaign page shows it and
  lets the player type one in.
- FIXED DICE: the save file keeps where the dice are. Reload and do the
  same thing: same result. Do something else first and the result may
  differ.
- Every rule roll is recorded with a label (who, what, odds, result). The
  test bench reads this log. The player sees the roll log only after the
  battle, on the debrief.

**Shot odds**
- One rule gives both the number shown and the number rolled. No hidden
  limits: 100 always hits, 0 always misses. (Today the roll adds plus or
  minus 10 for height and clamps to 5 to 95, and the three screens that
  show odds do neither.)
- The odds use: the weapon's own range (distance matters per weapon), what
  is between shooter and target, the soldier's stats and traits, and the
  kind of aim. Height stays in. (Today distance does not change the odds
  at all.)
- Things in the way (low wall, smoke, a tree, another unit) each lower the
  odds. A fully blocked line cannot be shot at. A missed shot just misses;
  it does not fly on and hit something else.
- The aiming screen shows one final number, with no breakdown. Auto fire
  shows the per-round odds times three, for example "38% x3".

**Battle rules**
- Both sides send the same commands (move, fire, overwatch, end turn and
  so on). The alien side is just another player. This lets the test bench
  put a scripted player on either side and lets a person play the aliens
  later.
- The rules take a mission, a squad and a seed, accept commands, and give
  back events (a plain record of what happened). Sound, effects, the screen
  log and the bench report all read the events.
- Built in slices, each leaving the game running: shooting and damage; then
  movement and overwatch; then morale; then alien moves; then Reach and
  explosives.
- A finished battle hands the campaign one outcome record.
- Battle replays: planned for, not built now. The event list is designed so
  a finished battle can be played back, possibly with a cinematic camera.
  To settle: what the cinematic view is.
- *Designer's call, agreed by the owner:* the rules resolve each action at
  once and the picture plays it back at its own speed, so frame rate cannot
  change an outcome.

**Saves**
- Normal play: save at any moment, many slots, plus an autosave at the
  start of each turn.
- IRON MAN MODE: one save slot, saved by the game after every decision.
  When the campaign ends, won or lost, the save is kept as a finished
  record that can be looked at but not played on.
- IRON MAN MODE and FIXED DICE are chosen on the new-campaign page, next to
  the seed, and are locked for the whole campaign.
- Each save records the game version. Until version 1.0 there is no
  promise: an old save that no longer fits is refused with a clear message,
  never loaded half-broken. From 1.0 on, old saves are upgraded.
- *Designer's call, agreed by the owner:* campaign state (only what a save
  holds) is separated from window, sound, screen and debug things.
- *Designer's call, agreed by the owner:* campaign actions (hire, build,
  research, make, buy, sell) move out of the drawing code into one place.
- *Designer's call, agreed by the owner:* things are found by id, not by
  position in a list.

Also to settle:
- What the cinematic replay view is.
- How many save slots there are.
- Which things in the way lower the odds, and by how much.

### Sixth session (2026-11-13): the character designer and ribbons

The owner decided how soldiers are made on the soldier pool page, and how
soldiers specialise. Nothing is built. All amounts are set with the test
bench, then checked by playing.

**Character designer**
- The designer is driven by hand: a detailed face (many sliders) and a
  simple body (build, height within a small range, skin, hair style and
  colour, and a voice).
- A photo is optional. It sets the face sliders for the player, in fine
  detail. The photo is read once and thrown away. Only slider values are
  saved. Nothing private is stored.
- A soldier can be made with no photo at all.
- The soldier's 3D head is built from the slider values. The close-up
  portrait is a picture of that head.
- This replaces the earlier rule "one picture per soldier, with the six
  stages laid over it" (third session, "The soldier pool"). Wear, the six
  portrait stages and event marks now apply to the head and its portrait.
- The player also sets: name, nickname, a short background (a line or two,
  flavour only), and a favoured role. The favoured role gives a head start
  on one ribbon. It changes no stats. Traits stay random and hidden each
  campaign (already decided).
- A pool soldier who dies in a campaign stays in the pool for future
  campaigns. Each campaign starts them clean: no ribbons carry over. The
  pool page keeps their full service record: every ribbon and citation
  ever earned, campaigns served, times fallen.
- Build order: the hand-driven sliders first, the photo step second.
- Research note (facts from a web check on 2026-11-13, to re-check before
  use):
  - MPFB2 is an open slider-driven human maker for Blender. Its add-on
    code is GPL-3.0 and its assets are CC0. It is the best open base.
  - No open photo-to-slider tool was found. That mapping is ours to
    write: find face landmarks in the photo, then set sliders to match.
  - The MediaPipe face landmarker has Apache-2.0 code. The licence of its
    model file is NOT yet checked.
  - The FLAME 2023 Open head model is CC BY 4.0. Every other FLAME version
    is academic only.
  - Traps: most well-known photo-to-3D-head research code depends on
    non-commercial face data (Basel Face Model, Surrey model, older
    FLAME), and several face model weight files are non-commercial.
  - The head is built once from slider values when a soldier loads, so the
    engine needs nothing per frame.

**Ribbons (how soldiers specialise)**
- Ribbons replace jobs. There are no classes and no fixed path. A soldier
  grows by what they do, again and again.
- Ribbons are worn on the dress uniform, on a ribbon board. Each ribbon
  has five levels. A higher level replaces the lower one on the board, so
  each level needs its own design.
- Ten ribbons to start:
  - rifle
  - shotgun
  - knife
  - explosives
  - medic
  - scout (spotting enemies first)
  - overwatch (reaction shots)
  - Reach
  - survivor (living through heavy damage gives a defence bonus over time)
  - creatures
- Creature ribbons: kills for beasts and machines. Captures for people
  (Tender, Lash, Regent): taking them alive earns the ribbon. Saving
  citizens on missions also earns a ribbon. Reason: living freed Tenders
  make the finale easier, so the ribbons reward what the story wants.
- Ribbon names and designs are to settle.
- Only uses that matter count (healing a real wound, hitting an enemy, a
  grenade that hurts something), with less gained for repeating within one
  battle.
- Reaching the top level should take the whole campaign, not the first
  three months. The target numbers for each level are set with the test
  bench, then checked by playing.
- **Hidden abilities (citations).** A mix of ribbons unlocks a special
  ability the player can stumble into. The owner accepted these four as "a
  good starting point". Exact effects are to settle with the test bench.
  - RANGER (rifle plus scout): sees further, and gets one overwatch shot
    each turn that costs no time units.
  - BREACHER (shotgun plus explosives): the first shot after going through
    a door or a blown wall costs no time units.
  - GUARDIAN (medic plus overwatch): a free reaction shot at anything that
    attacks a soldier they healed this battle.
  - GHOST (knife plus scout): enemies that have not spotted them cannot
    take reaction shots at them, and they leave no footprints.
- More hidden abilities are wanted. Which ones is to settle.
- A hidden ability stays hidden until one of the player's soldiers earns
  it. Then it appears in a book of citations with how it was earned, and
  stays known in every later campaign.

**Footprints**
- Units leave footprints that show in bad weather: snow, rain and the
  like. A GHOST leaves none.
- To settle: who can read tracks (soldiers, aliens or both), how long they
  last, which weather and ground count.

Also to settle:
- Ribbon names and designs.
- More hidden abilities, and the exact effects of the four above.
- How ribbons and citations relate to the earned traits already decided
  (shell shock, competence and the rest).
- The face slider list.
- The licence of the face landmark model file.
- Who reads footprints, how long they last, which weather and ground
  count.

### Seventh session (2026-11-13): stories players find themselves

The owner decided six ways to give players their own stories on missions.
Nothing is built. Anything not written here is to settle.

**1. Ribbon history**
- Every ribbon level remembers where and when it was earned: the mission,
  the date, and the deed that tipped it over to that level.
- The ribbon board reads like the soldier's history.

**2. Bonds**
- Soldiers form bonds by fighting side by side: shared missions, or one
  saving or healing the other.
- A bonded partner nearby steadies morale. A partner being hurt or killed
  hits morale hard.
- To settle: all the numbers (how fast a bond forms, how much morale moves,
  how near is "nearby").
- To settle: how a bond is shown to the player.

**3. Losing a bonded partner**
- It could go either way. A roll decides whether the survivor is shaken
  for some missions, or hardened with a bonus.
- To settle: which dice make that roll (campaign or battle), the odds, how
  many missions it lasts, and what the bonus is.

**4. Moment of the battle**
- The debrief picks one moment per battle from the roll log. Examples: a
  long-odds shot that hit, a rescue on the last time units, a lone
  survivor.
- It is shown with the soldier's name. It is also saved to that soldier's
  service record.
- To settle: how the moment is picked.

**5. Memorial wall**
- A screen in the base lists every fallen soldier: how, where and when they
  died, their ribbon board, and who they were bonded to.
- It is kept in the finished record when a campaign ends.

**6. Deployments**
- A pool soldier's past campaigns are shown on their record. They are
  called DEPLOYMENTS (the owner's word).
- To confirm: reading "Deployments" as the name for a pool soldier's past
  campaigns is the assistant's reading of the owner's short answer.
- Returning veterans also get a small visible nod for their deployments,
  for example a mark on the dress uniform. It has no effect on stats.
- To settle: the design of the mark.

## How we work on lore

The user designs the lore with Claude; nothing here is fixed without them.
Sessions are question rounds (up to four questions, recommended answer
first); each round's answers are written into this file straight away.

The user feeds the stories we settle to ChatGPT to visualise them and sends
the pictures back. Those pictures are idea references: they steer the
description here, and our own models are made from the descriptions, not
copied from the pictures. A sheet with several options does not mean
several units.
